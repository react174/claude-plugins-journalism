# Sports Subsidy Types: Definitions and Accounting

A guide to identifying, defining, and correctly accounting for different types of public subsidies given to sports teams and facilities.

---

## Direct Cash Grants

**Definition**: Government provides money directly to a team, stadium authority, or developer with no repayment obligation.

**Sources**: State economic development agencies, city general funds, federal grant programs (CDBG, EDA).

**How to account**: Full grant amount is the public cost. Check for conditions (jobs created, clawbacks if benchmarks not met).

**Common pitfalls**:
- Grants are sometimes spread over multiple years; total the full commitment, not just the first year
- "Forgivable loans" are grants if repayment is contingent on conditions that are routinely waived

---

## Tax-Exempt Municipal Bonds

**Definition**: Government issues bonds on behalf of a stadium project; interest paid to bondholders is exempt from federal (and often state) income tax. This allows the issuer to offer lower interest rates.

**Who benefits**: The borrower (team or stadium authority) pays lower interest. The federal government foregoes tax revenue on bond interest.

**How to account**:
- The **federal tax subsidy** is the foregone tax revenue (estimated as the difference between the tax-exempt rate and equivalent taxable rate, times the interest paid)
- The **direct public cost** depends on who pays debt service:
  - If the team pays: primarily a tax subsidy, not a local government expenditure
  - If a public authority pays with dedicated taxes: the dedicated tax revenue is the public cost
  - If backed by general fund: full debt service is public cost

**Common pitfalls**:
- Bond face value ≠ public cost. A $1 billion bond issuance may cost taxpayers $0 (if team pays all debt service) or $1 billion+ including interest (if government pays)
- Always identify the debt service structure

---

## Tax Increment Financing (TIF)

**Definition**: Government designates a TIF district around a project. Property tax revenues above a baseline ("the increment") are redirected to pay off bonds financing the project, rather than flowing to schools and other taxing bodies.

**How to account**:
- The public cost is the diverted tax increment over the life of the TIF
- Schools, parks, and other taxing bodies lose the increment for the TIF's duration
- TIF projections are often optimistic; actual diversions may differ

**Common pitfalls**:
- TIF "pays for itself" arguments ignore opportunity cost (the increment that would otherwise fund schools)
- Multi-decade TIFs: account for full duration, not just early years

---

## Property Tax Exemptions and Abatements

**Definition**: Publicly owned stadium land is typically exempt from property taxes. Tax abatements reduce or eliminate property taxes for private developers.

**Types**:
- **Full exemption**: Government-owned property (stadium on public land) is tax-exempt
- **Payment in lieu of taxes (PILOT)**: Owner pays an agreed amount instead of market-rate taxes
- **Abatement**: Tax reduction for a fixed term to incentivize development

**How to account**:
- Calculate taxes that would be owed at market rate
- Subtract any PILOT payments actually made
- Difference = annual tax subsidy
- Over the life of the deal: multiply annual subsidy by years remaining

**Common pitfalls**:
- Don't double-count with TIF—some of the "foregone" taxes would have gone to TIF increment anyway
- Get actual assessor data on what neighboring comparable properties pay

---

## Infrastructure Subsidies

**Definition**: Government funds infrastructure improvements that primarily benefit a stadium/team: roads, transit, utilities, parking garages, pedestrian infrastructure.

**How to account**:
- Identify which infrastructure is stadium-specific vs. genuinely public benefit
- Public projects with dual use (e.g., a road that also serves other businesses) should be prorated
- Document funding source: federal aid, state capital budget, local capital budget

**Common pitfalls**:
- "The road had to be improved anyway" is often asserted but rarely proven
- Consider counterfactual: would this infrastructure have been built without the stadium?

---

## Sales Tax Exemptions

**Definition**: Construction materials or operations are exempted from sales taxes normally applied to businesses.

**Common forms**:
- Construction material exemptions during stadium build
- Vendor sales inside stadium exempt from city/county sales tax
- Team merchandise sales exempt from sales tax

**How to account**: Estimate the tax that would have been collected on equivalent purchases.

---

## Hotel/Rental Car/Tourism Taxes

**Definition**: Dedicated taxes on hotel rooms, rental cars, or other tourism-related spending are pledged to pay stadium debt service or operating costs.

**How to account**:
- These are real public revenues, even if marketed as "paid by visitors"
- The public opportunity cost: these revenues cannot fund other public services
- Compare: what else could this revenue fund?

**Common pitfalls**:
- "Visitors pay for it" framing obscures that the public is still choosing to dedicate revenue to stadiums rather than other uses
- Hotel tax revenues are volatile; stress-test projections against economic downturns

---

## Operating Subsidies

**Definition**: Ongoing government payments to cover stadium operating costs, typically through a stadium authority.

**Common forms**:
- Annual management fees paid to team for operating a public stadium
- Government-funded capital repairs and maintenance
- Police, fire, and emergency services provided at no charge

**How to account**:
- Identify all annual payments in stadium authority budgets and city budgets
- Include in-kind services (security, sanitation) valued at cost to government

---

## Naming Rights Revenue Sharing

**Definition**: When government owns a stadium and shares naming rights revenue with the occupying team, or when a team keeps all naming rights revenue for a publicly financed stadium.

**How to account**:
- If government owns the facility, naming rights revenue should flow to the public
- If team keeps revenue from naming rights to a public asset, that foregone revenue is a subsidy

---

## Lease Terms Below Market

**Definition**: Teams lease publicly owned facilities at below-market rates—often $1/year or nominal rent—for decades.

**How to account**:
- Identify comparable commercial real estate lease rates
- Calculate the difference between market rate and actual payment
- Multiply by lease term

**Common pitfalls**:
- Lease agreements often include non-cash concessions (stadium improvements, profit sharing) that complicate comparisons
- Get the full lease and any amendments; term sheets in press releases are often incomplete

---

## Clawbacks and Conditions

**What they are**: Contractual provisions requiring repayment or compliance if the team fails to meet benchmarks (jobs, attendance, staying in the city).

**Why they matter**:
- Existence of clawbacks suggests government acknowledged risk at the time of the deal
- Whether clawbacks have ever been triggered (and enforced) is a key data point
- Clawback provisions vary widely in enforceability

**What to find**: The original subsidy agreement (not the press release), any amendments, and any history of enforcement actions.

---

## Calculating Total Public Cost

There is no single universally accepted methodology for calculating "total public subsidy." Be explicit about what you include and exclude.

**Components to consider including**:
- Direct cash grants (full amount)
- Debt service on public bonds (over life of bonds, or net present value)
- Foregone property taxes (estimated, over life of deal)
- TIF increment diverted (estimated, over TIF term)
- Infrastructure spending (stadium-attributable portion)
- Below-market lease value (estimated, over lease term)
- Ongoing operating subsidies

**Components typically excluded** (but worth noting):
- Federal tax subsidy from tax-exempt bonds (often reported separately as "federal taxpayer cost")
- Economic impact claims (these are projections, not public outlays)

**Always disclose**:
1. What you included and why
2. What you excluded and why
3. Date range covered
4. Key assumptions (discount rate if using NPV, comparable rent rates, etc.)
5. Primary sources for each component
